Project 2 · Inventory control

Warehouse Inventory Health, Replenishment & Cost

A snapshot of 3,204 warehouse items, used to decide which items to reorder now, which are overstocked, what the excess costs to hold, and how much a higher service level would cost.

Data: Logistics Warehouse Dataset (Kaggle, ziya07), a public synthetic dataset for 2024. The currency is not stated in the data card, so costs are in dataset units. The results demonstrate the method; they are not findings about a real warehouse.

Method

  1. Lead-time demand = daily demand × lead time (days).
  2. Safety stock = z × std dev of daily demand × √lead time, with z = 1.645 for a 95% service level.
  3. Reorder point (ROP) = lead-time demand + safety stock. Maximum level = ROP + daily demand × the item's reorder interval.
  4. Status: REORDER if stock ≤ ROP, OVERSTOCK if stock > maximum level, otherwise OK.
  5. ABC by annual demand value (80% / 95% cut-offs); XYZ by coefficient of variation (≤ 0.25 / ≤ 0.5 / above).

Why recalculate the reorder point?

The dataset ships with its own reorder point and reorder flag. In 76% of items, that reorder point is lower than the demand expected during the lead time, before any safety stock. It also has no relationship with the recalculated ROP (correlation −0.02).

A reorder point below lead-time demand guarantees a stockout before the next delivery arrives, so the dataset's flag cannot be trusted as-is.

The service level is an input cell in the workbook's PARAMETER sheet. Change it and every ROP, status, and cost figure updates.

1 · The dataset's reorder flag misses most items that need ordering

Number of items by status: recalculated vs the dataset's own flag (which only has reorder yes/no)

Recalculated, 944 items need reordering. The dataset flags only 240, so 727 items that should be ordered are treated as safe. It also has no overstock category, while 1,207 items sit above their maximum level.

2 · Overstock carries half the holding cost

Share of items vs share of daily holding cost, by recalculated status

Holding cost totals about 881,500 per day (about 26.4 million per 30 days). Overstocked items are 37.7% of items but 49.3% of that cost. Bringing them down to their maximum level would cut about 199,900 per day (22.7% of holding cost, about 6.0 million per 30 days) and release about 21.1 million of stock value (23.5% of inventory value).

3 · Each step up in service level costs more than the last

Daily holding cost of safety stock across all items, by target service level

Going from 90% to 95% raises the daily cost of safety stock by 28.3%. Going from 95% to 99% adds another 41.4%. The z-value grows faster than the service level, so the last few percent of availability are the most expensive. A sensible policy sets a high level only where the item's value or criticality justifies it.

4 · ABC-XYZ matrix: one policy does not fit all items

Number of items by value class (ABC) and demand stability (XYZ). Darker = more items.

AX (1,213 items): high value, stable demand, so routine replenishment with small safety stock. AZ (24): high value and unstable, so these need the closest monitoring. CZ (421): low value and unstable, so candidates for minimum stock or order-on-demand.

Slotting check

Item popularity has no relationship with picking time (correlation 0.01). Of the 839 most popular items (top 25%), 422 sit in locations with above-median picking time. These are the first candidates to move closer to the dispatch area. Average picking time is almost the same across zones (93.8 to 96.7 seconds), so the issue is item placement, not one slow zone.

Left out on purpose

  • "KPI drivers": the data card says KPI_score is calculated from fulfillment, stockouts, layout, turnover, and holding cost, so correlating them would only repeat the formula.
  • Stockout count: it has no relationship with stock, lead time, or cover days in this synthetic data.
  • EOQ: the dataset has no ordering cost, so it cannot be calculated honestly.
